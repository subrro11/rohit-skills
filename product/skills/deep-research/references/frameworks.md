# Frameworks

A framework is a way to arrange evidence so a decision becomes obvious. It isn't evidence itself. Pick one to three that fit the decision, fill every cell with ledger IDs or mark it *inference*, and turn empty cells into "Evidence needed:" lines.

## Pick by decision

| Decision | Use |
|---|---|
| Is this market worth entering? | Five forces, then trends (PESTLE) |
| Where do we win against rivals? | Positioning map, then jobs to be done |
| What should the wedge or first feature be? | Jobs to be done, then the competitor gap table |
| Will this lead last? | Moats |
| Is the company healthy, or what will it do next? | Business model, then news |
| What could blindside us? | Trends (PESTLE), then the disconfirming searches |

## Jobs to be done

**Shape:** "When <situation>, I want to <motivation>, so I can <outcome>." Then list the current solutions hired for that job, including doing nothing.
**Needs:** user signal (forums, reviews, interviews) and the competitor table.
**Watch out for:** a job written as a feature ("I want a planner"). The job should hold even if every current product disappeared.

## Positioning map

**Shape:** two axes that buyers actually choose on, with each competitor placed and cited. The empty quadrant is a candidate position, and the report must say why it's empty: either nobody has tried it, or nobody wants it.
**Needs:** what each competitor leads with (their own site or store listing) and price.
**Watch out for:** axes chosen to make your product look alone. Say where the buyer evidence for each axis comes from.

## Five forces

**Shape:** one line per force (rivalry, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, buyer power, supplier power), each rated low, medium or high with evidence.
**Needs:** competitor count and traction, switching costs, price pressure, dependence on platforms or suppliers (app stores, APIs, data sources).
**Watch out for:** rating a force with no evidence. "High rivalry" needs the competitor rows behind it.

## Trends (PESTLE)

**Shape:** political, economic, social, technological, legal and environmental rows. Fill only the rows that touch the decision. For each trend give its direction, its evidence and what it means for the decision.
**Needs:** stage 4 evidence, with at least two independent sources per trend.
**Watch out for:** filling all six rows. Most decisions turn on one or two.

## Business model

**Shape:** customer, value proposition, channel, revenue model and price, key costs, and the one metric the business lives on.
**Needs:** stage 1 evidence: pricing pages, filings, docs, job posts.
**Watch out for:** assuming a revenue model from the product. If no source states it, mark it *inference*.

## Moats

**Shape:** for each candidate advantage (network effects, switching costs, scale economies, brand, proprietary data or process, regulation), say whether it exists now, the evidence, and how a rival could copy or route around it.
**Needs:** news (who has tried to copy it), competitor traction, switching-cost evidence from user signal.
**Watch out for:** calling an early lead a moat. A moat says why a rival who copies the product still loses.

## Competitor gap table

**Shape:** rows are the jobs or features buyers care about, columns are competitors, cells say supported, partly supported or not supported, each with a source. The useful row is the one that's empty across every competitor *and* backed by user signal.
**Needs:** each competitor's own docs or store listing, plus hands-on testing when possible.
**Watch out for:** "not supported" based on a listing that didn't mention the feature. Absence from a listing is weak evidence; mark it Low.
