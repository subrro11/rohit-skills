# Writing tasks

A task is a short scenario that gives someone a reason to use the product and everything they need to act, and nothing that tells them how. If the wording does the thinking for them, you learn nothing about the design.

## What a good task has

1. **A goal from their life**, not a feature tour. "You've just finished a night shift" beats "Open the check-in screen".
2. **The details they need**: dates, names, amounts, what's in the fridge. Put long details on a card instead of in the sentence.
3. **An action to perform**, not a question about what they'd do. Watching beats asking, because people are poor reporters of their own behaviour.
4. **A clear end**: the moderator can tell when it's done, and so can the participant.
5. **No interface words.** Don't use the labels on buttons, tabs or menus. If the screen says "Re-plan", the task says "update your plan" or "sort out your week".
6. **No steps.** "Go to settings, then…" turns a test into a dictation.
7. **One goal per task.** Two goals in one task means you can't tell which one failed.

## Good and leading, side by side

| Leading | Why it leads | Better |
|---|---|---|
| Tap **Log prep** and add your dishes. | Names the button and the step | Here's what's in your fridge. Get it into the app. |
| Use the **Re-plan** button to rebuild your week. | Uses the label; tells them the feature exists | Thursday has changed from a day shift to a night shift. Update your plan. |
| How easy was it to find the use-by list? | Assumes they found it; asks for a rating, not behaviour | Find out which of your prepped food can't be used this week. |
| Would you use this to plan your meals? | Yes/no, asks them to predict | Tell me about the last time your rota changed. What happened to the food you'd prepped? (warm-up) |
| Check that the plan uses your fridge food first. | States the answer they should find | Which meals this week use food you've already prepped? (comprehension) |
| Try the new check-in feature. | Feature tour, no goal | You've just finished a night shift. Tell the app what you ate. |

## Order and setup

- Put tasks in the order a real person would meet them. Start with something achievable so nerves settle.
- Write a **starting state** for each task: which screen is showing and what data is loaded.
- Write a **reset** for between participants (clear storage, reload with a fixed date) so everyone starts the same.
- Keep the riskiest task early enough that it's never the one cut when time runs short.

## Before you use a task

Read it aloud to someone who hasn't seen the product. If they ask "where do I click?", it's working. If they ask "what do you mean?", add a detail. If they can guess the screen from the wording, rewrite it.

## Probes, after the task

Ask these once the task is finished or abandoned, not during it.

- "What were you expecting to happen there?"
- "You paused on that screen. What was going through your mind?"
- "How would you normally do this today?"
- "Was anything missing that you looked for?"

Never: "Did you see the button at the bottom?", "Was that easy?", "Would you like it if…?"
